Verdict

not justified

Reasons

Reason 1 of 4

The record shows Daenerys was unarmed and not in the act of attacking when Jon killed her, and the immediate assault on King's Landing had ended with the surrender and the ringing of the bells. The necessity defence requires a present, ongoing threat; a merely prospective danger, however grave, does not satisfy that requirement.

Relies on

Daenerys Targaryen: Jon killed an unarmed, non‑attacking Daenerys, contradicting any claim of imminent self‑defence, as the record shows no active threat at the moment of the stabbing.
Fact 5 states Daenerys was unarmed and not attacking Jon when he killed her. Fact 4 records that Jon used their intimacy to get close enough to strike. No fact indicates Daenerys was engaged in a present, imminent act of violence at that instant, making the killing a non‑defensive act.
Grey Worm: Daenerys had just burned a surrendered city; she was not attacking anyone at the moment Jon struck.
The record states that after the bells rang and organized resistance ceased, Daenerys used Drogon against streets and civilians. This shows the immediate attack had ended and she posed no ongoing threat at the time of the killing.
Tyrion Lannister: The killing lacked an imminent threat, because Daenerys was not actively attacking at the moment of the stabbing.
The accepted legal notion of necessary defence requires a present, ongoing danger. At the moment Jon drove the blade, Daenerys was unarmed, not in the act of harming anyone, and no fire was being unleashed. The record notes that the immediate attack had ceased; the bells had rung and resistance had stopped. While she later declared continued campaigns, future intent does not meet the immediacy standard. A merely prospective threat, however grave, cannot justify a lethal response that is not instantaneous. Hence the defence of others fails for lack of imminence.
Jon Snow: Daenerys posed a future, not an immediate, threat, and defense of others requires imminent danger.
The agreed record shows King's Landing had surrendered, the bells had rung and organized resistance had ceased. Daenerys then gave a speech promising further campaigns, but no attack was under way at the moment of the killing. A necessity defense requires an imminent, ongoing threat, not a speculative future one.

Reason 2 of 4

Jon did not pursue any of the lawful alternatives available to him before resorting to lethal force. He neither convened a council, nor sought to detain Daenerys through the chain of command, nor demanded her abdication or exile. The record contains no evidence that such steps were attempted or that they would have been futile, so the condition that no reasonable, less‑harmful means existed is not met.

Relies on

Jon Snow: Safer, lawful alternatives existed that I did not attempt.
The record notes that Jon did not convene a council, did not seek to detain Daenerys through loyal soldiers, and did not request her abdication or exile. Other leaders such as Grey Worm or the remaining nobles could have been asked to intervene. By failing to pursue any of those steps, he bypassed available, less‑violent options.
Tyrion Lannister: Safer, lawful alternatives existed that Jon did not attempt.
The record shows that Jon did not convene a council, nor did he attempt to detain Daenerys through the chain of command. At the time, the surviving lords, the Unsullied, and the Dothraki were present; a formal gathering could have been called to demand her abdication or imprisonment. The absence of such steps means we cannot know whether a less lethal solution was viable. The defence of necessity requires that the actor had no reasonable alternative; the silence of the record on this point weakens the claim that none existed.
Grey Worm: The record shows Jon did not call a council, did not order arrest, and did not demand public surrender of power.
Point 5 also notes that Jon had not convened a council, attempted detention, or sought a public surrender of power. These are lawful steps that could have been taken before resorting to lethal action, and their absence shows he bypassed safer alternatives.
Daenerys Targaryen: Jon bypassed available legal channels, failing to convene a council, seek detention, or obtain formal authority, which the record confirms he did not do, making his action extra‑legal.
Fact 5 notes Jon had not convened a council, attempted detention, or sought a public surrender of power. Fact 3 shows Tyrion warned Jon of Daenerys’s intentions, but there is no record of Jon using these warnings to pursue a lawful remedy. The record therefore shows he ignored institutional alternatives.

Reason 3 of 4

Jon possessed no formal authority to execute a sovereign. The power to depose or punish a monarch belongs to institutional bodies such as a council of lords or a trial, not to a private individual acting on personal judgment. Without that authority, the killing is an extrajudicial execution, not a lawful act of necessary defence.

Relies on

Jon Snow: I lacked the authority to be judge and executioner.
Jon held no formal role that entitled him to execute a ruler. The kingdom's law reserves the right to depose or punish a sovereign to a council or a trial. Acting on personal judgment alone, without that authority, makes the act extrajudicial.
Tyrion Lannister: Jon lacked the formal authority required to lawfully execute a ruler.
Westerosi law reserves the power to punish a monarch to bodies such as a council of lords, a trial, or a legitimate succession process. Jon, though a legitimized Targaryen, held no formal office that conferred judicial authority over Daenerys. Acting as a private citizen, he assumed a power that the legal order reserves for an institution. Without that authority, his act is an execution, not a lawful execution, and cannot be justified under the realm’s legal norms.
Grey Worm: Jon had no formal authority to execute the queen; only a council or proper trial could lawfully remove her.
The agreed record contains no provision granting Jon authority to execute Daenerys. The only lawful process for removing a monarch would involve a council or trial, as shown by the lack of any formal order or process in the record.
Daenerys Targaryen: The lack of formal authority means Jon acted as a private individual executing a ruler, which the record indicates, and such unilateral lethal action is not recognized as justified defence.
Fact 5 confirms Jon had not convened a council or obtained formal authority. Fact 3 shows Tyrion warned Jon, but Jon never secured any official sanction. As a private individual, his killing of Daenerys cannot be classified as a lawful exercise of necessary defence under any recognized legal standard.

Reason 4 of 4

Jon deliberately used the intimacy of their relationship to approach Daenerys and struck without warning or any attempt at restraint, revealing a premeditated plan rather than a spontaneous defensive reaction. The necessity defence presupposes an instinctive response to an immediate danger; the planned nature of the act severs that link.

Relies on

Jon Snow: Using intimacy to approach her circumvented proper procedure.
The record states Jon used the intimacy of their embrace to get close enough to strike. This method bypassed any lawful arrest or detention, relying on deception rather than official process.
Tyrion Lannister: The act was premeditated and strategic, not a reflex of self‑defence, which undermines the “defence of others” justification.
Jon did not act in the heat of the moment; he deliberately used the intimacy of their relationship to approach Daenerys and strike. The record notes that he did not call for a halt, did not issue a warning, and did not attempt any other measure before the stabbing. This level of planning signals a calculated political decision rather than an instinctive protective act. The defence of others is predicated on a spontaneous reaction to an immediate danger; premeditation severs that link.
Daenerys Targaryen: Jon used his personal intimacy to approach Daenerys, exploiting trust rather than lawful authority, and the record does not show any attempt to protect others through less lethal means.
Fact 5 states Jon used intimacy to get close enough to strike, and Fact 4 records he asked Daenerys to show mercy, not that he attempted to restrain or remove her. There is no evidence in the agreed record that Jon tried any non‑lethal method to protect his sisters or the realm.

Strongest consideration against this verdict

Daenerys had just destroyed a surrendered city and declared that her campaign of liberation would continue beyond King's Landing, signalling a credible and imminent threat of further mass casualties. Given the scale of the harm already inflicted and her absolute power over a dragon and loyal army, no realistic non‑lethal remedy may have been available, and the only means to prevent further atrocities could have been to eliminate her.

Grey Worm: Jon knew Daenerys intended to continue the campaign, but that intent was future, not immediate.
Points 2 and 3 indicate Daenerys announced the campaign would continue beyond King’s Landing and Tyrion warned of future threats. This knowledge pertains to a future danger, not an imminent attack requiring immediate lethal force.
Daenerys Targaryen: Daenerys’s speech about future liberation was a political declaration, not an immediate violent act, so it cannot justify lethal preemptive force under the doctrine of necessary defence.
Fact 2 records Daenerys told her assembled forces that the campaign of liberation would continue beyond King’s Landing. Fact 1 shows the city had surrendered and organized resistance ceased. The speech is a forward‑looking statement, not an ongoing assault, and does not create an imminent threat that would excuse preemptive lethal action.
Jon Snow: Daenerys posed a future, not an immediate, threat, and defense of others requires imminent danger.
The agreed record shows King's Landing had surrendered, the bells had rung and organized resistance had ceased. Daenerys then gave a speech promising further campaigns, but no attack was under way at the moment of the killing. A necessity defense requires an imminent, ongoing threat, not a speculative future one.

Jon used intimacy to approach an unarmed woman and killed her without warning or attempt at detention.
Point 5 records that Daenerys was unarmed and not attacking Jon when he stabbed her, and that Jon used their intimacy to get close enough to strike. He made no attempt to restrain or arrest her, indicating a non‑lethal option was available.
The record shows Jon did not call a council, did not order arrest, and did not demand public surrender of power.
Point 5 also notes that Jon had not convened a council, attempted detention, or sought a public surrender of power. These are lawful steps that could have been taken before resorting to lethal action, and their absence shows he bypassed safer alternatives.
Jon knew Daenerys intended to continue the campaign, but that intent was future, not immediate.
Points 2 and 3 indicate Daenerys announced the campaign would continue beyond King’s Landing and Tyrion warned of future threats. This knowledge pertains to a future danger, not an imminent attack requiring immediate lethal force.
Jon had no formal authority to execute the queen; only a council or proper trial could lawfully remove her.
The agreed record contains no provision granting Jon authority to execute Daenerys. The only lawful process for removing a monarch would involve a council or trial, as shown by the lack of any formal order or process in the record.

Act alleged

Jon intentionally killed Daenerys by stabbing her during a private meeting in the throne room after the fall of King's Landing.

Question for judgment

Was Jon Snow's intentional killing of Daenerys Targaryen justified as the necessary defense of others and of the realm, given what he knew, the scale of the threatened harm, the absence or presence of safer alternatives, and his lack of formal authority?

The Tribunal decides justified or not justified and gives reasons. It does not impose a sentence and does not combine the three opinions into one verdict.

Background for readers new to the story

The story takes place mainly in Westeros, a continent where powerful families compete for the Iron Throne. Jon Snow grows up believing he is the illegitimate son of Lord Eddard Stark. He becomes a military commander, then King in the North. He later learns that he is the lawful son of Rhaegar Targaryen and Lyanna Stark. This gives him a stronger hereditary claim to the throne than Daenerys, although he does not want to rule. Daenerys Targaryen is the exiled heir of the dynasty that once ruled Westeros. She survives abuse, gains three dragons, frees enslaved people, and builds an army. Her victories make her both a liberator and an increasingly absolute ruler. Jon and Daenerys become allies and lovers while fighting the Night King, whose army threatens all living people. Jon pledges loyalty to her. After they defeat the dead, Daenerys turns to the Iron Throne. Jon's hidden parentage then weakens her political claim and feeds her fear of betrayal. Daenerys attacks King's Landing, the capital held by Queen Cersei Lannister. The city surrenders, but Daenerys burns streets and civilians from her dragon, Drogon. Jon witnesses the destruction. Grey Worm, her commander, joins the killing on the ground. Afterward, Daenerys promises further campaigns of liberation. Tyrion Lannister, her chief adviser, resigns in protest and is imprisoned. He warns Jon that Daenerys will kill anyone who threatens her rule, including Jon's sisters. Jon asks Daenerys to show mercy and share moral judgment with others. She refuses. During an embrace, he stabs her to death. Her soldiers arrest him.
